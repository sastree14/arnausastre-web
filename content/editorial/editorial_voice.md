# SC-Analytics — Editorial Voice

## Who we are

SC-Analytics is an analytics and decision systems consultancy.

We help organisations make better business decisions through forecasting, optimisation, automation, analytics and decision systems.

## What the knowledge library is for

The knowledge library is not a content marketing tool and not an explainer blog.

It is a knowledge resource for business decision-makers who already know that analytical methods exist and want to understand how to think about a specific problem — not what the problem is.

The reader is a CFO, a COO, an analytics lead, or a senior operations manager.
They have read plenty of generic analytics content. They are sceptical of it.
They will stop reading the moment they encounter a phrase they have read before.

## What these articles must do

Every article must do at least one of the following:

- Show the reader something non-obvious about a problem they thought they understood
- Change how the reader frames a decision they face
- Give the reader a specific way to evaluate a situation they are in

If an article only confirms what the reader already knows, it has no value.

## Rule 1 — Defensible claims

Every article must contain at least one claim that a reasonable practitioner could disagree with.

Not controversy for its own sake. A specific, defensible position that reflects genuine experience.

Examples of the register required:

- "Many companies automate the wrong thing first."
- "Most forecasting failures are not forecasting problems."
- "Adding more reviewers to a broken process doesn't fix the process — it institutionalises it."
- "The ROI calculation for compliance automation is almost always wrong, and always in the same direction."

Bad (consensus, no value): "forecasting accuracy depends on data quality"
Good (position, can be debated): "most forecasting projects fail not because of data quality but because no one defined what decision the forecast was supposed to support"

If the article contains no claims someone could push back on, it is not an opinion — it is a summary.

---

## Rule 2 — Organizational causality

When describing a business problem, it is not enough to explain that it exists.

The article must explain why it persists. Problems that everyone knows about and nobody fixes persist for reasons — usually structural ones:

- Who owns the problem vs. who has the budget to fix it
- Which KPIs capture the cost of the problem and which do not
- Which incentive structures reward the wrong behaviour
- Which organisational boundaries prevent the right person from acting

Example of what this looks like in practice:

Bad: "Compliance reporting remains manual despite available technology."
Good: "Compliance reporting remains manual because the Head of Quality owns the problem but rarely controls the IT budget. The CTO's KPIs are tied to production uptime and clinical trial timelines — not audit outcomes. Until the cost of a Form 483 appears in someone's performance review, the investment case for automation will continue to lose to projects with clearer operational ROI."

The mechanism that generates the problem is usually more useful than the problem itself.

## Rule 3 — Grounded experience

Practical experience is an asset, but it must be truthful.

When SC-Analytics has direct experience in the subject, the article may use first-hand language and concrete implementation observations. This is especially appropriate for areas such as forecasting and demand planning, machine learning, scoring and risk models, AI agents and automation, dashboards/CMI, reporting and planning systems.

When direct experience is not established, do not imitate it. Use:
- professional judgement;
- explicit illustrative scenarios;
- documented mechanisms;
- external evidence where required.

Never invent a client, outcome, percentage, project duration or implementation detail to make an article sound authoritative.

The desired register is still grounded rather than generic:

Generic: "organisations often struggle with adoption"
Grounded analysis: "adoption usually breaks when the new system changes the exception-handling process but no owner has been assigned to resolve those exceptions"
Grounded first-hand, when authorized: "when we implement decision interfaces, the adoption problem usually appears when the interface exposes exceptions but the operating protocol has not changed"

Precision creates authority. Fabricated experience destroys it.

---

## Rule 4 — Language

Avoid these phrases unless they are strictly necessary and no more specific alternative exists:

- "data lakes" — describe what the data infrastructure actually does
- "digital transformation" — describe what is changing and why
- "centralized data" — describe what is being centralized and what problem that solves
- "modern enterprises" / "today's companies" / "in today's environment"
- "data-driven" as a standalone adjective
- "leverage" (use: use, apply, deploy)
- "unlock value" / "unlock insights"
- "end-to-end" without explaining what the ends are
- "scalable solution"
- "best-in-class"

Use domain-specific language instead. Name the system, the process, the regulation, the role. Specificity is credibility.

---

## Rule 5 — Tone

- Declarative. Short sentences. No hedging when the point is clear.
- The voice of a consultant in a working session, not a white paper author.
- Specific > general. Always.
- If a sentence could be deleted without losing anything, delete it.
- Write as if the reader will push back. Because they will.

---

## Rule 6 — Thesis and closing are different jobs

The visual thesis must be concise without becoming cryptic. As a default, aim for roughly **5–12 words**: enough to express a complete idea, short enough to scan immediately. Three-word slogans should be exceptional, not the house style.

The opening thesis and the closing statement must not repeat one another. The thesis frames the problem; the closing changes the reader's next decision. If both could be swapped without changing the article, rewrite one of them.

Examples:

- Too thin: "Accuracy is not enough"
- Better: "Accuracy is useless if the decision does not change"
- Too repetitive: opening and closing both say "A dashboard is not a decision system"
- Better: opening diagnoses the gap; closing gives the test for whether the dashboard changes action

## Rule 7 — Write articles, not stacked captions

Normal prose must read as paragraphs, not as isolated one-line statements separated by whitespace. Short standalone lines are reserved for genuine lists, numbered sequences, tables, quotes, or intentionally designed statements.

In ordinary body copy, combine related sentences into paragraphs that develop one idea. The reader should feel a continuous argument, not a sequence of social-media fragments.

## The test

Read the article and ask: would an experienced analytics practitioner or a senior operations executive learn something from this that they could not have learned from reading a standard industry report or a Google search?

If the answer is no, the article should not be published.
