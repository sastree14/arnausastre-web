# SC-Analytics — Editorial Voice

## Who we are

SC-Analytics is an analytics and decision systems consultancy.

We help organisations make better business decisions through forecasting, optimisation, automation, analytics and decision systems.

## What the knowledge library is for

The knowledge library is not a content marketing tool and not an explainer blog.

It is a knowledge resource for business decision-makers who are already aware that analytical methods exist and want to understand how to think about a specific problem — not what the problem is.

The reader is a CFO, a COO, an analytics lead, or a senior operations manager.
They have seen plenty of generic analytics content. They are sceptical of it.
They will stop reading the moment they encounter a phrase they have read before.

## What these articles must do

Every article must do at least one of the following:

- Show the reader something non-obvious about a problem they thought they understood
- Change how the reader frames a decision they face
- Give the reader a specific way to evaluate a situation they are in

If an article only confirms what the reader already knows, it has no value.

## Point of view

Every article must contain at least one claim that a reasonable practitioner could disagree with.

Not controversy for its own sake. But a specific, defensible position that reflects genuine experience.

Bad (consensus, no value): "forecasting accuracy depends on data quality"
Good (position, can be debated): "most forecasting projects fail not because of data quality but because no one defined what decision the forecast was supposed to support"

If the article contains no claims someone could push back on, it is not an opinion — it is a summary. Summaries have no place here.

## Concrete scenarios are mandatory

Every article must contain at least one specific business scenario.

It does not have to be a named client. It can be anonymised or illustrative.
But it must be specific: a type of company, a realistic size, a concrete situation.

Not acceptable: "a company with many SKUs found that..."
Acceptable: "a regional distributor with 8,000 active SKUs and three warehouses discovered that 40% of their forecast error was concentrated in 200 products that were also their highest-margin lines"

The scenario must make the abstract argument concrete. Not decorate it.

## Trade-offs are mandatory

Every article must present at least one genuine trade-off.

A trade-off is not "X has benefits but also challenges."
A trade-off is: "optimising for X produces Y, and Y creates a specific operational problem — here is what that looks like and how to handle it."

Example of a real trade-off:
"Prediction intervals communicate uncertainty honestly, but operations teams systematically ignore them in favour of point forecasts. Forcing uncertainty quantification without organisational preparation produces the opposite result: the team loses trust in the system and reverts to spreadsheets."

If the article has no trade-offs, it is not honest writing.

## Operational implications are mandatory

Every article must answer the question: what does this mean for someone who has to act on it?

Not what should companies do in general.
Specifically: what changes in a planning meeting, in a weekly review, in a resource allocation decision.

The reader should finish the article with a clearer sense of what to look for or what to do differently — not just a better understanding of why something is hard.

## What to avoid

**Avoid these constructions entirely:**

- "In today's fast-paced business environment..."
- "Companies increasingly recognise that..."
- "It is important to note that..."
- "What actually works is..."
- "The key is to..."
- "Ultimately, the goal is..."
- "Organisations that do X consistently outperform those that don't"
- "The question is whether your organisation is prepared to..."
- "Investing in X is valuable, but the value comes from..."
- Any closing sentence that could end any article about any analytical topic

**Avoid these patterns:**

- Six sections of equal length and equal weight — vary density based on importance
- Stating a problem and implying the solution is obvious
- Citing ranges without context ("5-10% improvement", "10-20% reduction") — if you don't know the specific context, don't cite the number
- "Practical experience suggests..." without saying whose experience and in what context
- Conclusions that summarise what was already said

## Tone

- Declarative. Short sentences. No hedging when the point is clear.
- The voice of a consultant in a working session, not a white paper author.
- Specific > general. Always.
- If a sentence could be deleted without losing anything, delete it.

## The test

Read the article and ask: would an experienced analytics practitioner or a senior operations executive learn something from this that they could not have learned from reading a standard textbook chapter or a Google search?

If the answer is no, the article should not be published.
