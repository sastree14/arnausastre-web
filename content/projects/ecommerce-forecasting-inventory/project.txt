Context:
A high-growth e-commerce operation with over 2,100 active SKUs across multiple product categories was making inventory decisions using a combination of manual rules, historical averages and planner judgement. The business was scaling faster than the planning process could handle. Stockouts on high-margin products and excess inventory on seasonal lines were occurring simultaneously — a pattern that typically indicates the planning system is operating at the limits of manual capacity.

Problem:
The core problem was not a lack of data. The operation had detailed sales history, supplier lead times, and warehouse capacity constraints. The problem was that none of this information was being used in a structured way to produce forecasts at the SKU level across multiple planning horizons.

Planners were producing weekly estimates for roughly 400 priority SKUs manually. The remaining 1,700+ SKUs were being replenished on fixed reorder points set months earlier. The result was a system that was reactive by design: it responded to stockouts after they happened rather than anticipating them.

Approach:
The project began with a data audit across three fulfilment systems to understand what information was available, at what granularity, and with what reliability. Several data quality issues were identified and resolved before any modelling began — including inconsistent promotional tagging that was distorting baseline demand estimates.

The forecasting system was built around a hierarchical ensemble combining statistical seasonality decomposition at the category level with gradient boosting models trained on SKU-level features. The features included price elasticity proxies, promotional flags, lead time variability, and cross-category demand signals.

Rather than optimising for a single accuracy metric, the system was calibrated against a cost-weighted error function that penalised stockouts on high-margin SKUs more heavily than overstock on low-margin lines.

System Developed:
The delivered system produces probabilistic forecasts at 30, 91, and 182-day horizons for all 2,100+ active SKUs. Outputs include point estimates and 80th and 95th percentile prediction intervals, enabling planners to set safety stock levels analytically rather than by rule of thumb.

A Monte Carlo inventory simulation layer runs 250 demand scenarios per SKU, producing a distribution of inventory positions and associated costs for each replenishment decision. This allows the planning team to evaluate the expected cost of different service level targets before committing to an order.

The system is integrated into the operation's ERP via an API layer and produces a daily planning file with flagged exceptions — SKUs where the forecast has changed materially, where inventory is projected to breach a threshold, or where the model's uncertainty has increased.

Results:
The system improved demand forecast accuracy from a WAPE of 0.48 to 0.31 across the full SKU catalogue. The largest accuracy gains were on seasonal and promotional lines, where the old system had no mechanism for anticipating demand shifts.

Planner time spent on manual exception handling dropped significantly as the system automated the identification of SKUs requiring intervention. The planning team shifted from producing forecasts to reviewing and acting on exceptions — a change that took three months to embed operationally.

Confidentiality:
Client name, internal data, specific product categories and operational details have been anonymised. Metrics reflect real system performance on anonymised data.
